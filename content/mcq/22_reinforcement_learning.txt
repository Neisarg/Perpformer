Q: A Markov decision process is defined by [E]
- states and rewards only
* states, actions, transition probabilities, a reward function and a discount factor
- a dataset of labeled examples
- a policy and a value function only
> The Markov property means the next state and reward depend only on the current state and action.

Q: With discount factor gamma = 0.9 and a constant reward of 1 at every step forever, the return is [E]
- 1
- 9
* 10
- infinite
> The geometric series sums to 1 / (1 - gamma) = 10.

Q: The Bellman optimality equation for Q* is [M]
- Q*(s, a) = r + gamma * mean over a' of Q*(s', a')
* Q*(s, a) = E[r + gamma * max over a' of Q*(s', a')]
- Q*(s, a) = max over s' of r
- Q*(s, a) = V*(s) for every a
> The optimal action value equals the expected immediate reward plus the discounted value of acting optimally afterwards.

Q: Q-learning is called off-policy because [M]
- it does not use a policy
* its update target uses the greedy action in the next state, regardless of the action the behaviour policy actually takes
- it learns from a fixed dataset only
- it requires a model of the environment
> The max in the target evaluates the greedy policy while data can come from an exploratory policy. SARSA, which uses the action actually taken, is on-policy.

Q: Experience replay in DQN is used to [M]
- increase the reward
* break the correlation between consecutive samples and reuse past transitions
- compute policy gradients
- avoid using a neural network
> Sampling random minibatches from a buffer makes the data closer to i.i.d. and improves sample efficiency.

Q: The target network in DQN [M]
- chooses actions during exploration
* is a periodically updated copy of the Q-network used to compute stable bootstrap targets
- stores the replay buffer
- predicts the reward function
> Without it the targets move with every update, which can cause oscillation or divergence.

Q: Double DQN reduces which problem of standard DQN? [H]
- Slow environment simulation
* Over-estimation of Q-values caused by taking a max over noisy estimates
- Sparse rewards
- Catastrophic forgetting of the policy network's architecture
> The online network selects the next action and the target network evaluates it, decoupling selection from evaluation.

Q: The policy gradient theorem gives the gradient of expected return as [M]
- the gradient of the reward function with respect to actions
* E[grad log pi(a|s) * (return or advantage)]
- the difference between two Q-values
- the Hessian of the value function
> The score-function estimator needs no gradient of the environment dynamics, only of the policy's log-probabilities.

Q: Subtracting a baseline, such as a state-value estimate, from the return in REINFORCE [M]
- introduces bias and reduces variance
* reduces variance without introducing bias
- increases variance
- changes the optimal policy
> Any baseline that does not depend on the action has zero expected gradient contribution, so the estimator stays unbiased while variance drops.

Q: In an actor-critic method, the critic [E]
- selects actions
* estimates a value function used to evaluate the actor's actions
- stores the environment model
- sets the discount factor
> The actor is the policy; the critic's value estimates give lower-variance learning signals (advantages) than Monte Carlo returns.

Q: PPO's clipped surrogate objective is designed to [M]
- maximize the entropy of the policy only
* prevent excessively large policy updates by clipping the probability ratio between new and old policies
- remove the need for a value function
- make learning off-policy with a replay buffer
> Clipping the ratio to [1 - eps, 1 + eps] keeps the new policy close to the data-collecting policy, approximating a trust region with first-order optimization.

Q: Generalized advantage estimation (GAE) with parameter lambda trades off [H]
- exploration and exploitation
* bias and variance of the advantage estimate, between one-step TD (lambda = 0) and Monte Carlo (lambda = 1)
- actor and critic learning rates
- on-policy and off-policy data
> GAE is an exponentially weighted sum of TD residuals: small lambda relies on the (biased) critic, large lambda on (noisy) returns.

Q: Epsilon-greedy exploration means [E]
- always taking a random action
* taking a random action with probability epsilon and the greedy action otherwise
- adding noise to the reward
- decaying the learning rate
> Epsilon is often annealed from high to low so the agent explores early and exploits later.

Q: The Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) algorithm for bandits selects the arm with the highest [M]
- observed mean reward
* mean reward plus an uncertainty bonus that shrinks as the arm is pulled more often
- number of pulls
- most recent reward
> "Optimism in the face of uncertainty" gives logarithmic regret by exploring arms whose values are still uncertain.

Q: Thompson sampling chooses actions by [M]
- always picking the arm with the highest posterior mean
* sampling a value for each arm from its posterior and picking the arm with the highest sample
- picking arms uniformly at random
- maximizing an upper confidence bound
> Probability matching explores in proportion to the chance each arm is optimal and is widely used in online experimentation.

Q: Monte Carlo and temporal-difference value estimation differ in that TD [M]
- must wait until the end of an episode
* bootstraps from the current value estimate of the next state, allowing updates after every step
- has higher variance and no bias
- cannot be used with function approximation
> TD targets r + gamma V(s') have lower variance but are biased by the current estimate; Monte Carlo returns are unbiased but high-variance.

Q: The "deadly triad" that can make value learning diverge consists of [H]
- exploration, exploitation and discounting
* function approximation, bootstrapping and off-policy training
- actor, critic and replay buffer
- reward shaping, clipping and normalization
> Each alone is fine, but together they can cause unbounded value estimates, motivating target networks and other stabilizers.

Q: Offline (batch) RL is hard mainly because of [H]
- slow simulators
* distribution shift: the learned policy queries actions outside the dataset, where value estimates are unreliable
- too much exploration
- the lack of rewards
> Bootstrapping from over-estimated out-of-distribution actions compounds errors; methods such as CQL and IQL keep the policy close to the data or penalize unseen actions.

Q: Behaviour cloning suffers from compounding errors because [M]
- the expert is suboptimal
* small mistakes lead the agent to states not covered by expert demonstrations, where it errs more
- it uses rewards incorrectly
- it requires a model of the environment
> Supervised imitation assumes i.i.d. states; DAgger addresses this by querying the expert on states visited by the learner.

Q: Reward shaping with a potential-based function F = gamma * phi(s') - phi(s) [H]
- can change the optimal policy arbitrarily
* leaves the optimal policy unchanged while possibly speeding up learning
- makes all rewards zero
- applies only to bandits
> Ng, Harada and Russell showed that potential-based shaping preserves optimal policies; other shaping can introduce unintended behaviour.

Q: An entropy bonus in the policy objective (as in SAC or A3C) [M]
- makes the policy deterministic
* encourages exploration by discouraging premature collapse to a deterministic policy
- increases the discount factor
- replaces the critic
> Maximum-entropy RL rewards randomness in addition to return, improving exploration and robustness.

Q: Model-based RL differs from model-free RL in that it [E]
- uses no neural networks
* learns or uses a model of environment dynamics to plan or to generate simulated experience
- cannot handle continuous actions
- needs no reward
> Planning with a model (as in MuZero or Dreamer) can be far more sample-efficient, at the risk of exploiting model errors.

Q: In the contextual bandit setting, compared with full RL, [M]
- there are no rewards
* actions do not affect future states, so there is no long-term credit assignment
- the action space is always continuous
- the policy is fixed
> Each round is an independent context-action-reward interaction, a good model for recommendations and ad selection.

Q: Importance sampling is used in off-policy evaluation to [H]
- increase exploration
* reweight returns collected under a behaviour policy to estimate the value of a different target policy
- reduce the size of the replay buffer
- normalize observations
> Weights are products of pi_target / pi_behaviour over the trajectory; they are unbiased but can have very high variance.

Q: Sparse rewards make learning difficult mainly because [M]
- the discount factor must be zero
* the agent rarely observes any learning signal, so credit assignment and exploration are hard
- gradients explode
- the state space becomes continuous
> Remedies include reward shaping, curiosity-driven exploration, hindsight experience replay and curricula.
