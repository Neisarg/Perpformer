# topic: CNNs & Computer Vision
# id: vision
# subtopics: Convolution arithmetic (stride, padding, dilation); Receptive field and pooling; Classic architectures (ResNet, Inception, MobileNet, EfficientNet); Object detection (anchors, IoU, NMS, mAP); Segmentation; Vision Transformers; Contrastive and self-supervised vision

Q: A 32 x 32 input passes through a convolution with kernel 5, stride 1, padding 0. The spatial output size is [E]
- 32 x 32
- 30 x 30
* 28 x 28
- 27 x 27
> Output = floor((n + 2p - k) / s) + 1 = (32 - 5) / 1 + 1 = 28.

Q: A conv layer has 64 input channels, 128 output channels and 3 x 3 kernels with bias. Its parameter count is [E]
- 1,152
- 73,728
* 73,856
- 8,192
> 3 * 3 * 64 * 128 = 73,728 weights plus 128 biases = 73,856.

Q: Which property do convolutional layers build in that fully connected layers lack? [E]
- Rotation invariance
* Translation equivariance through weight sharing and local connectivity
- Scale invariance
- Permutation invariance
> The same kernel is applied at every position, so shifting the input shifts the feature map. Parameter sharing also reduces the number of weights dramatically.

Q: Two stacked 3 x 3 convolutions (stride 1) have the same receptive field as [M]
- one 3 x 3 convolution
* one 5 x 5 convolution
- one 6 x 6 convolution
- one 9 x 9 convolution
> Each additional 3 x 3 layer adds 2 to the receptive field: 3, then 5. Stacking small kernels uses fewer parameters and adds non-linearities.

Q: A 1 x 1 convolution is mainly used to [M]
- increase spatial resolution
* change the number of channels and mix information across channels cheaply
- enlarge the receptive field
- replace pooling
> It is a per-pixel linear layer over channels, used in bottlenecks (ResNet, Inception) to reduce or expand channel dimension.

Q: A depthwise separable convolution factorizes a standard convolution into [M]
- two 1-D convolutions along height and width
* a per-channel spatial (depthwise) convolution followed by a 1 x 1 (pointwise) convolution
- a convolution and a max-pool
- a dilated and a transposed convolution
> This reduces parameters and computation roughly by a factor of the kernel area, and is the basis of MobileNet and Xception.

Q: Dilated (atrous) convolutions [M]
- reduce the number of parameters by pruning
* enlarge the receptive field without adding parameters or reducing resolution
- up-sample the feature map
- are equivalent to strided convolutions
> Inserting gaps between kernel elements widens the area covered; they are popular in segmentation (DeepLab) and audio models (WaveNet).

Q: The key idea introduced by ResNet is [E]
- using only 1 x 1 convolutions
* identity shortcut connections so layers learn residual functions
- removing all pooling layers
- replacing convolutions by attention
> Residual connections solved the degradation problem, allowing networks with hundreds of layers to train effectively.

Q: Intersection over Union (IoU) of two boxes is [E]
- the area of their union
* the area of their intersection divided by the area of their union
- the distance between their centres
- the ratio of their areas
> IoU ranges from 0 (disjoint) to 1 (identical) and is used for matching predictions to ground truth and in NMS.

Q: Non-maximum suppression (NMS) in object detection [M]
- increases the number of predicted boxes
* keeps the highest-scoring box and removes other boxes that overlap it above an IoU threshold, repeatedly
- merges all boxes into one
- is applied to the ground-truth boxes
> NMS removes duplicate detections of the same object. Soft-NMS decays scores instead of deleting boxes.

Q: Two-stage detectors such as Faster R-CNN differ from one-stage detectors such as YOLO or RetinaNet in that they [M]
- do not use convolution
* first generate region proposals and then classify and refine each proposal
- predict only one box per image
- cannot be trained end to end
> Two-stage models are typically more accurate on small objects but slower; one-stage models predict classes and boxes densely in one pass.

Q: Mean average precision (mAP) in detection is computed by [M]
- averaging accuracy over images
* averaging, over classes (and often IoU thresholds), the area under each class's precision-recall curve
- taking the mean IoU of all boxes
- counting boxes per image
> Detections are ranked by confidence; AP summarizes the PR curve per class. COCO mAP averages over IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95.

Q: A transposed convolution is commonly used to [M]
- reduce spatial resolution
* up-sample feature maps with learnable weights, for example in decoders and generators
- compute the transpose of the weight matrix for backprop only
- replace batch normalization
> It maps each input element to a kernel-sized output patch; uneven overlap can cause checkerboard artifacts.

Q: In U-Net, the skip connections between encoder and decoder [M]
- reduce the number of parameters
* carry high-resolution features to the decoder so fine spatial detail is recovered
- replace the loss function
- are used only at inference time
> Down-sampling loses localization information; concatenating encoder features lets the decoder combine semantics with detail.

Q: A Vision Transformer (ViT) processes an image by [M]
- applying self-attention to every pixel separately
* splitting it into fixed-size patches, linearly embedding them, adding position embeddings and feeding the sequence to a Transformer encoder
- running a CNN followed by an RNN
- convolving with learned Gabor filters only
> A 224 x 224 image with 16 x 16 patches becomes 196 tokens (plus a class token).

Q: Compared with CNNs, Vision Transformers trained from scratch on small datasets tend to [M]
- generalize better because they have stronger inductive biases
* perform worse because they lack convolutional inductive biases and need more data or strong augmentation
- train without position information
- need no normalization
> Locality and translation equivariance must be learned from data; large-scale pretraining or DeiT-style recipes close the gap.

Q: Global average pooling before the classifier, instead of flattening into large fully connected layers, [M]
- increases the number of parameters
* removes most classifier parameters and allows variable input sizes
- prevents the use of residual connections
- makes the network translation variant
> Averaging each channel over space gives a fixed-length vector regardless of resolution and acts as a structural regularizer.

Q: The dice loss is popular for segmentation because [M]
- it is identical to cross-entropy
* it directly optimizes region overlap and is robust to foreground/background imbalance
- it requires bounding boxes
- it ignores false negatives
> Dice = 2|A and B| / (|A| + |B|). With tiny foreground regions, pixel-wise cross-entropy is dominated by background.

Q: In contrastive self-supervised learning such as SimCLR, a positive pair is formed by [M]
- two different images of the same class label
* two random augmentations of the same image
- an image and random noise
- an image and its label embedding
> No labels are used; the model learns to pull augmented views together and push other images in the batch apart (InfoNCE loss).

Q: CLIP is trained by [M]
- classifying images into 1,000 classes
* contrastively aligning image and text embeddings of matching image-caption pairs
- reconstructing masked image patches
- generating captions token by token
> A symmetric contrastive loss over a batch makes matched image-text pairs similar, enabling zero-shot classification with text prompts.

Q: Max pooling with a 2 x 2 window and stride 2 on a feature map [E]
- doubles the spatial size
* halves the height and width and keeps the strongest activation in each window
- has learnable parameters
- changes the number of channels
> Pooling down-samples spatially, adds small translation invariance and has no parameters.

Q: Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN) improve detection of objects at different scales by [H]
- training separate networks per scale
* combining low-resolution, semantically strong features with high-resolution features through a top-down pathway and lateral connections
- resizing the input image many times at inference
- using larger kernels
> Each pyramid level gets strong semantics at its own resolution, so small objects are detected on fine maps and large ones on coarse maps.

Q: For a convolution with kernel size k, to keep the output the same spatial size as the input at stride 1 ("same" padding, odd k), the padding must be [E]
- k
* (k - 1) / 2
- k / 2 + 1
- 0
> Output = n + 2p - k + 1 = n requires p = (k - 1) / 2, for example p = 1 for a 3 x 3 kernel.

Q: Masked autoencoders (MAE) for vision pretrain by [H]
- contrasting two augmented views
* masking a large fraction of patches (around 75%) and reconstructing the missing pixels from the visible ones
- predicting image rotations
- classifying patches by position
> A high masking ratio makes the task non-trivial and the encoder efficient, since it only processes the visible patches.

Q: Anchor boxes in detectors like Faster R-CNN and RetinaNet are [M]
- ground-truth annotations
* predefined reference boxes of several scales and aspect ratios, relative to which offsets are predicted
- the final detections after NMS
- pixels with the highest activation
> The network classifies each anchor and regresses offsets to fit objects. Anchor-free detectors (FCOS, CenterNet) predict boxes from points instead.
