Q: A disease affects 1% of people. A test has 99% sensitivity and 95% specificity. Given a positive result, the probability of having the disease is closest to [M]
- 99%
- 95%
- 50%
* 17%
> P(D|+) = 0.99*0.01 / (0.99*0.01 + 0.05*0.99) = 0.0099 / 0.0594, about 0.167. With a rare condition, false positives dominate.

Q: Maximum likelihood estimation of the mean of a Gaussian with known variance gives [E]
- the sample median
* the sample mean
- the sample mode
- the midpoint of min and max
> Maximizing the Gaussian log-likelihood is minimizing the sum of squared deviations, which is achieved by the sample mean.

Q: The MLE of the variance of a Gaussian, (1/n) * sum (x_i - mean)^2, is [M]
- unbiased
* biased low by a factor (n-1)/n
- biased high by a factor n/(n-1)
- inconsistent
> E[MLE variance] = (n-1)/n * sigma^2. Dividing by n-1 (Bessel's correction) gives the unbiased estimator; the MLE is still consistent.

Q: MAP estimation with a zero-mean Gaussian prior on the weights of a linear regression model is equivalent to [E]
- L1 regularization (lasso)
* L2 regularization (ridge)
- Early stopping
- Dropout
> The negative log of a Gaussian prior is proportional to ||w||^2, which adds an L2 penalty to the negative log-likelihood. A Laplace prior gives L1.

Q: If X and Y are independent, which statement is always true? [E]
* Cov(X, Y) = 0
- Var(X + Y) = Var(X) - Var(Y)
- E[X/Y] = E[X]/E[Y]
- X and Y have the same distribution
> Independence implies zero covariance (the converse is false). Variances add for independent variables.

Q: Zero correlation between X and Y implies independence [M]
- always
- never
* when (X, Y) are jointly Gaussian
- when X and Y are both uniformly distributed
> Uncorrelated does not generally imply independent (take Y = X^2 with symmetric X), but for jointly Gaussian variables it does.

Q: A p-value of 0.03 means [M]
- there is a 3% probability that the null hypothesis is true
- there is a 97% probability that the alternative is true
* if the null hypothesis were true, data at least this extreme would occur 3% of the time
- the effect size is 0.03
> The p-value is P(data at least as extreme | H0). It is not the posterior probability of H0, and says nothing by itself about effect size.

Q: The central limit theorem states that, for i.i.d. variables with finite variance, as n grows [E]
- each sample becomes normally distributed
* the standardized sample mean converges in distribution to a normal
- the sample variance goes to zero
- the sample maximum becomes normal
> The CLT is about the distribution of the (scaled) sample mean, not about the individual observations.

Q: The standard error of the mean of n i.i.d. samples with standard deviation sigma is [E]
- sigma
- sigma / n
* sigma / sqrt(n)
- sigma^2 / n
> Var(mean) = sigma^2 / n, so its standard deviation is sigma / sqrt(n). Quadrupling the data halves the error.

Q: KL(P || Q) is [M]
- symmetric and always non-negative
* non-negative and zero only when P = Q, but not symmetric
- a metric satisfying the triangle inequality
- bounded above by 1
> KL divergence is non-negative by Gibbs' inequality, asymmetric, and can be infinite when Q assigns zero probability where P does not.

Q: Minimizing cross-entropy H(P, Q) over Q, with the data distribution P fixed, is equivalent to minimizing [M]
- H(Q)
- KL(Q || P)
* KL(P || Q)
- the mutual information between P and Q
> H(P, Q) = H(P) + KL(P || Q), and H(P) does not depend on Q. This is why maximum likelihood equals minimizing forward KL.

Q: Which discrete distribution over K outcomes has the maximum entropy? [E]
- A one-hot (deterministic) distribution
* The uniform distribution
- A geometric distribution
- A Zipfian distribution
> Entropy is maximized at log K by the uniform distribution and minimized at 0 by a deterministic one.

Q: The number of events in a fixed interval, when events occur independently at a constant average rate, is modeled by [E]
- a Bernoulli distribution
- an exponential distribution
* a Poisson distribution
- a beta distribution
> Counts follow a Poisson; the waiting times between such events follow an exponential distribution.

Q: The conjugate prior for the success probability of a Bernoulli/binomial likelihood is [M]
- Gaussian
- Gamma
- Dirichlet over three outcomes
* Beta
> With a Beta(a, b) prior and s successes in n trials, the posterior is Beta(a + s, b + n - s). The Dirichlet generalizes this to multinomials.

Q: You run 20 independent hypothesis tests at significance level 0.05 when all null hypotheses are true. The probability of at least one false positive is about [M]
- 5%
- 23%
* 64%
- 100%
> 1 - 0.95^20 is about 0.64. This is the multiple comparisons problem, addressed by Bonferroni or FDR corrections.

Q: A 95% confidence interval for a parameter means [M]
- the parameter lies in this particular interval with probability 0.95
* the procedure produces intervals that contain the true parameter in 95% of repeated experiments
- 95% of the data falls inside the interval
- the estimate is within 5% of the truth
> Frequentist coverage is a property of the procedure. A probability statement about the parameter itself requires a Bayesian credible interval.

Q: Type I and Type II errors are, respectively, [E]
* rejecting a true null; failing to reject a false null
- failing to reject a false null; rejecting a true null
- both kinds of false negatives
- errors in the prior and errors in the likelihood
> Type I is a false positive (rate alpha); Type II is a false negative (rate beta). Power is 1 - beta.

Q: The law of total variance states Var(Y) = [H]
- Var(E[Y|X])
- E[Var(Y|X)] - Var(E[Y|X])
* E[Var(Y|X)] + Var(E[Y|X])
- Var(Y|X) * Var(X)
> Total variance splits into the expected within-group variance plus the variance of the group means (unexplained plus explained).

Q: Jensen's inequality for a convex function f and random variable X states [M]
- f(E[X]) >= E[f(X)]
* f(E[X]) <= E[f(X)]
- f(E[X]) = E[f(X)]
- E[f(X)] <= 0
> For convex f the function of the mean is at most the mean of the function. It underlies the ELBO and the non-negativity of KL.

Q: To sample from an arbitrary 1-D distribution with invertible CDF F using uniform random numbers u ~ U(0,1), you compute [M]
- F(u)
* F^-1(u)
- 1 - F(u)
- u * F(1)
> Inverse transform sampling: if U is uniform then F^-1(U) has CDF F.

Q: The bootstrap estimates the sampling distribution of a statistic by [M]
- sampling without replacement half of the data many times
* resampling the dataset with replacement many times and recomputing the statistic
- adding Gaussian noise to every data point
- fitting a Gaussian and sampling from it
> The non-parametric bootstrap draws n points with replacement from the n observed points. Each resample contains about 63.2% of the unique original points.

Q: Mutual information I(X; Y) is zero if and only if [M]
- X and Y are uncorrelated
* X and Y are independent
- X is a deterministic function of Y
- H(X) = H(Y)
> I(X; Y) = KL(p(x, y) || p(x) p(y)), which vanishes exactly when the joint factorizes. Unlike correlation it captures non-linear dependence.

Q: You flip a fair coin until the first head. The expected number of flips is [E]
- 1
- 1.5
* 2
- infinite
> The count is geometric with p = 0.5, whose mean is 1/p = 2.

Q: Which of the following makes a t-test preferable to a z-test for a mean? [M]
- The sample size is very large
* The population variance is unknown and estimated from a small sample
- The data are binary
- More than two groups are compared
> Using the sample standard deviation adds uncertainty; the t distribution's heavier tails account for it. With large n the two tests coincide. Several groups call for ANOVA.

Q: For a model that outputs class probabilities, the negative log-likelihood of the data under a categorical distribution is the same as [E]
- mean squared error
- hinge loss
* cross-entropy loss
- the 0-1 loss
> Cross-entropy with one-hot targets is exactly -log p(correct class), the categorical negative log-likelihood.
