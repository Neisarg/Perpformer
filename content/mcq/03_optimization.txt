# topic: Optimization
# id: optim
# subtopics: Gradient descent and SGD; Momentum and Nesterov; Adaptive methods (RMSProp, Adam, AdamW); Learning-rate schedules and warmup; Convexity, saddle points and curvature; Second-order methods; Batch size and gradient noise

Q: Compared with full-batch gradient descent, mini-batch SGD [E]
- computes exact gradients more cheaply
* uses noisy gradient estimates that are much cheaper per update
- always converges to a better minimum
- cannot be used with momentum
> A mini-batch gradient is an unbiased but noisy estimate of the full gradient, giving many cheap updates per pass over the data.

Q: In SGD with momentum, the velocity update v <- mu * v + g primarily [E]
- normalizes each parameter's gradient by its variance
* accumulates an exponentially weighted sum of past gradients, damping oscillations
- decays the weights toward zero
- computes second derivatives
> Momentum averages gradients over time: consistent directions build speed while oscillating components cancel.

Q: Adam's bias correction (dividing by 1 - beta^t) exists because [M]
- gradients are biased estimates of the true gradient
* the moment estimates are initialized at zero and are biased toward zero early in training
- the learning rate must decay over time
- weight decay biases the weights
> With m_0 = v_0 = 0, the exponential moving averages underestimate the true moments for small t; the correction rescales them.

Q: What distinguishes AdamW from Adam with L2 regularization added to the loss? [M]
- AdamW uses Nesterov momentum
- AdamW drops the second-moment estimate
* AdamW applies weight decay directly to the weights, decoupled from the adaptive gradient scaling
- AdamW uses a larger epsilon
> With L2 in the loss, the decay term is divided by sqrt(v), so parameters with large gradients are decayed less. AdamW decouples decay from the adaptive step.

Q: With Adam's default hyper-parameters, the size of an individual parameter's update is roughly bounded by [H]
- the gradient magnitude
- the squared gradient
* the learning rate
- the weight magnitude
> The step is lr * m_hat / (sqrt(v_hat) + eps), and |m_hat| / sqrt(v_hat) is about 1 or less, so each step is on the order of lr regardless of gradient scale.

Q: Learning-rate warmup is used with Transformers and adaptive optimizers mainly because [M]
- it reduces the total number of training steps
* early updates are unstable: second-moment estimates are unreliable and large steps can cause divergence
- it prevents overfitting at the end of training
- it removes the need for weight decay
> At the start, Adam's variance estimates are based on few samples and the network is far from a stable regime; small initial steps avoid divergence.

Q: For a convex and L-smooth function, gradient descent is guaranteed to decrease the objective at each step if the step size is at most [M]
- L
* 1/L
- 2L
- sqrt(L)
> With step size eta <= 1/L the descent lemma gives f(x_next) <= f(x) - (eta/2)||grad||^2. Steps above 2/L can diverge.

Q: In high-dimensional non-convex losses of deep networks, critical points with high loss are most often [M]
- global minima
- poor local minima
* saddle points
- local maxima
> For a random critical point in high dimensions it is very unlikely that all Hessian eigenvalues are positive, so most are saddles; gradient noise helps escape them.

Q: Newton's method is rarely used directly to train large neural networks because [E]
- it needs the loss to be linear
* forming and inverting the Hessian costs O(n^2) memory and O(n^3) time in the number of parameters
- it cannot handle more than one layer
- it requires a batch size of 1
> With millions or billions of parameters the Hessian is intractable; quasi-Newton or diagonal approximations are used instead.

Q: Gradient clipping by global norm [E]
- clips each gradient element to [-c, c] independently
* rescales the whole gradient vector when its norm exceeds a threshold, preserving its direction
- sets small gradients to zero
- normalizes every gradient to unit norm at every step
> If ||g|| > c the gradient is multiplied by c / ||g||. Direction is preserved, unlike element-wise value clipping.

Q: Which optimizer divides the learning rate by the square root of the sum of ALL past squared gradients, causing the effective learning rate to shrink monotonically? [M]
- RMSProp
* Adagrad
- Adam
- SGD with momentum
> Adagrad accumulates squared gradients without decay, so its step sizes only shrink. RMSProp and Adam fix this with an exponential moving average.

Q: Which is a convex function of w? [M]
- The loss of a two-layer ReLU network
* Logistic regression's negative log-likelihood
- sin(w)
- -||w||^2
> Logistic loss is a composition of a convex function (log-sum-exp) with a linear map. Neural network losses are non-convex in their weights.

Q: Increasing the batch size by a factor k, the "linear scaling rule" suggests [M]
- dividing the learning rate by k
* multiplying the learning rate by k
- leaving the learning rate unchanged
- squaring the learning rate
> Larger batches give lower-variance gradients, and k small-batch steps are approximated by one large step with a k-times larger learning rate (usually with warmup).

Q: Cosine annealing of the learning rate [E]
- increases the learning rate following a cosine up to a maximum
* decays the learning rate smoothly from its initial value toward a minimum along half a cosine wave
- oscillates the learning rate between negative and positive values
- applies only to momentum
> lr_t = lr_min + 0.5 (lr_max - lr_min)(1 + cos(pi t / T)). It decays slowly at first, fastest in the middle, and gently at the end.

Q: Nesterov momentum differs from classical momentum in that it [M]
- uses second-order information
* evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead position after applying the momentum step
- keeps a per-parameter learning rate
- resets velocity every epoch
> Nesterov computes the gradient at theta + mu * v, giving a corrective effect that improves convergence for smooth convex problems.

Q: The gradient noise of mini-batch SGD is often argued to help generalization because it [M]
* biases optimization toward flatter minima
- guarantees reaching the global minimum
- removes the need for regularization
- makes the loss convex
> Noise scale grows with lr / batch size; noisy dynamics tend to escape sharp minima, and flat minima are associated empirically with better generalization.

Q: A training loss that oscillates wildly or becomes NaN within the first few hundred steps most likely indicates [E]
- the model is too small
* the learning rate is too high
- the batch size is too large
- too much weight decay
> Divergence early in training is the classic symptom of an excessive step size; lower it or add warmup and gradient clipping.

Q: For an ill-conditioned quadratic (very different curvatures along different directions), plain gradient descent [M]
- converges in one step
* zig-zags along steep directions and progresses slowly along flat ones
- converges faster than on a well-conditioned problem
- cannot converge for any step size
> The step size is limited by the largest curvature, so progress along low-curvature directions is slow. Momentum and preconditioning address this.

Q: L1 regularization tends to produce sparse solutions because [M]
- its gradient grows with the weight magnitude
* its penalty has a constant-magnitude (sub)gradient that pushes small weights exactly to zero
- it is differentiable everywhere
- it penalizes large weights more than L2
> The L1 subgradient has magnitude lambda regardless of weight size, so weights are driven to exactly zero, whereas L2's shrinkage vanishes as weights get small.

Q: In RMSProp, each parameter's update is the gradient divided by [E]
- the running mean of past gradients
* the square root of a running average of squared gradients
- the parameter's current value
- the Hessian diagonal computed exactly
> RMSProp normalizes by an exponential moving average of squared gradients, giving a per-parameter adaptive step size.

Q: The Lagrangian dual and KKT conditions are most directly associated with [M]
- training decision trees
* constrained optimization, such as the SVM margin problem
- stochastic gradient descent with momentum
- batch normalization
> Constrained problems are analysed with Lagrange multipliers; the SVM dual and its support vectors come from the KKT complementary slackness conditions.

Q: Which statement about second-order information is correct? [H]
- A large positive Hessian eigenvalue indicates a flat direction
* The largest Hessian eigenvalue limits the stable learning rate of gradient descent to about 2 / lambda_max
- Negative Hessian eigenvalues indicate a local minimum
- The Hessian of a neural network loss is always diagonal
> For a quadratic, gradient descent is stable only if lr < 2 / lambda_max. Large eigenvalues are sharp directions; negative ones indicate descent directions at a saddle.

Q: Exponential moving averages (EMA) of model weights are kept during training in order to [M]
- speed up backpropagation
* evaluate or sample with a smoothed set of weights that often generalizes better
- reduce GPU memory use
- replace the optimizer's momentum buffer
> Averaging iterates reduces noise from the last SGD steps. It is standard in diffusion models and semi-supervised learning.

Q: Which problem does the "one-cycle" or warmup-then-decay schedule address compared with a constant learning rate? [M]
- It removes the need to shuffle data
* It allows large learning rates in the middle of training while keeping the start stable and the end fine-grained
- It guarantees convexity
- It makes batch normalization unnecessary
> Small rates at the start avoid divergence, high rates mid-training speed progress and regularize, and low rates at the end let the model settle.

Q: Coordinate descent, proximal gradient and ADMM are optimization methods most relevant when [H]
- the objective is a deep network trained end to end
* the objective has non-smooth terms (such as an L1 penalty) or separable structure
- only zeroth-order information is available
- the problem has no constraints and is strongly convex and tiny
> Proximal and splitting methods handle non-differentiable regularizers and constraints; for example, lasso is solved by coordinate descent or ISTA with soft-thresholding.
