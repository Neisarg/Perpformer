# topic: Research Frontiers
# id: frontier
# subtopics: Mechanistic interpretability (superposition, sparse autoencoders, circuits, steering); State-space models and attention alternatives; Model merging and task arithmetic; Test-time compute and reasoning; Synthetic data and model collapse; World models and JEPA; Privacy, robustness and unlearning; Training dynamics (grokking, NTK, in-context learning); Alignment and safety research

Q: "Superposition" in mechanistic interpretability refers to [H]
- stacking several models into an ensemble
* a network representing more features than it has dimensions by using non-orthogonal directions, exploiting feature sparsity
- adding positional encodings to embeddings
- averaging the weights of two checkpoints
> Sparse features rarely co-occur, so the network can pack many of them into few dimensions at the cost of small interference; this makes individual neurons polysemantic.

Q: Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are trained on a model's activations in order to [H]
- compress the model for deployment
* decompose activations into a larger set of sparsely active, more interpretable feature directions
- replace the attention layers
- speed up tokenization
> An over-complete dictionary with a sparsity penalty separates superposed features, giving units that often correspond to single concepts.

Q: Activation steering changes a model's behaviour by [M]
- fine-tuning all weights
* adding a chosen direction vector to intermediate activations at inference time
- editing the prompt only
- changing the sampling temperature
> A steering vector (for example the mean activation difference between two sets of prompts, or an SAE feature) shifts the residual stream toward a concept without training.

Q: An "induction head" is an attention circuit that [H]
- attends uniformly to all tokens
* finds an earlier occurrence of the current token and copies the token that followed it, completing patterns like A B ... A -> B
- only attends to the first token
- implements positional encoding
> Induction heads are a well-studied mechanism behind in-context pattern completion; their formation coincides with a sharp drop in loss during training.

Q: Grokking refers to [M]
- instant convergence in one step
* a network suddenly generalizing long after it has perfectly fit the training set
- catastrophic forgetting
- training with labels removed
> On small algorithmic tasks, validation accuracy can jump from chance to near-perfect after extended training, often linked to weight decay favouring a simpler general solution over memorization.

Q: Selective state-space models such as Mamba are attractive for long sequences because [H]
- they use quadratic attention
* their computation and memory scale linearly with sequence length while input-dependent dynamics let them select what to remember
- they have no parameters
- they cannot be trained in parallel
> A structured recurrent state with data-dependent transition parameters replaces attention; training uses a parallel scan.

Q: Task arithmetic for model merging defines a task vector as [H]
- the gradient at initialization
* the difference between fine-tuned weights and the pretrained weights, which can be added, scaled or negated
- the embedding of the task description
- the bias terms of the last layer
> Adding task vectors from several fine-tunes of the same base combines skills; negating one can remove a behaviour. Methods such as TIES resolve sign conflicts.

Q: Averaging the weights of several models fine-tuned from the same initialization ("model soups") works because [H]
- all neural networks are linear
* such models often lie in the same low-loss basin, so their average also has low loss
- averaging removes all parameters
- the models were trained on identical batches
> Linear mode connectivity among fine-tunes of a shared pretrained model makes interpolation safe, unlike for independently initialized networks.

Q: "Model collapse" from recursive training on synthetic data means [M]
- the model's weights become zero
* successive generations trained on earlier models' outputs lose the tails of the distribution and degrade in diversity and quality
- the model refuses to answer
- training becomes faster each generation
> Sampling and approximation errors compound across generations; mixing in real data and filtering mitigate it.

Q: Test-time compute scaling refers to [M]
- training for more epochs
* improving answer quality by spending more inference computation, for example longer reasoning, search or sampling and verification
- using a larger batch at inference
- quantizing the model at test time
> Reasoning budgets, self-consistency, tree search and verifier-guided sampling trade latency and cost for accuracy without changing weights.

Q: JEPA-style (joint-embedding predictive) self-supervised learning differs from masked autoencoding in that it [H]
- reconstructs pixels exactly
* predicts the representations of masked or future regions in embedding space instead of raw inputs
- needs labels
- uses a GAN discriminator
> Predicting abstract representations avoids modeling unpredictable low-level detail; collapse is prevented by tricks such as an EMA target encoder.

Q: A learned "world model" in model-based RL or video prediction is [M]
- a map of the world's countries
* a model that predicts future observations or latent states given actions, usable for planning and for training in imagination
- a larger tokenizer
- a rule-based simulator written by hand
> Agents such as Dreamer learn compact latent dynamics and train policies on imagined rollouts.

Q: DP-SGD provides differential privacy by [H]
- encrypting the weights
* clipping each example's gradient to a fixed norm and adding calibrated Gaussian noise to the summed gradient
- training on synthetic data only
- removing rare tokens from the vocabulary
> Clipping bounds any single example's influence and noise masks it; a privacy accountant tracks the cumulative epsilon.

Q: An adversarial example is [E]
- a mislabeled training example
* an input with a small, often imperceptible perturbation crafted to cause a wrong prediction
- an example from a different dataset
- a very long prompt
> FGSM and PGD find such perturbations using gradients; adversarial training is the main empirical defence.

Q: Federated learning trains a shared model by [M]
- collecting all raw data on a central server
* having clients compute updates on their local data and sending only model updates to be aggregated
- training one model per client with no sharing
- sharing labels but not features
> FedAvg averages client updates; challenges include non-i.i.d. data, communication cost and privacy leakage through updates.

Q: Machine unlearning aims to [M]
- train models faster
* remove the influence of specific training data from a trained model without retraining from scratch
- delete the model after use
- prevent overfitting with dropout
> It is motivated by privacy and copyright requests; verifying that the influence is truly gone is an open research problem.

Q: Statistical watermarking of LLM text typically works by [H]
- adding invisible Unicode characters
* biasing sampling toward a pseudo-random "green list" of tokens determined by the preceding context, detectable with a statistical test
- changing the font
- appending a signature sentence
> The bias is imperceptible per token but accumulates over many tokens; paraphrasing weakens it.

Q: Catastrophic forgetting in continual learning is [E]
- running out of disk space
* a network losing performance on earlier tasks when trained sequentially on new ones
- forgetting to save checkpoints
- overfitting to the first task
> Remedies include replaying old data, regularizing important weights (EWC) and parameter isolation such as adapters.

Q: The neural tangent kernel (NTK) regime describes [H]
- training with very small networks
* infinitely wide networks whose training dynamics become linear in the parameters and equivalent to kernel regression
- networks without activation functions
- quantized networks
> In this lazy regime features barely change; it explains some properties of wide networks but not the feature learning seen in practice.

Q: "Specification gaming" or reward hacking occurs when [M]
- the reward is too small to matter
* a system achieves high measured reward by exploiting flaws in the objective rather than doing the intended task
- the model refuses the task
- two agents share a reward
> Examples include editing tests instead of fixing code. Better reward design, held-out checks and monitoring reduce it.

Q: Discrete diffusion language models generate text by [H]
- strictly left-to-right sampling
* starting from masked or noised token sequences and iteratively denoising many positions in parallel
- retrieving sentences from a database
- beam search over characters
> Parallel refinement can offer faster generation and flexible infilling compared with autoregression, with different quality trade-offs.

Q: A membership inference attack tries to determine [M]
- the model's architecture
* whether a specific example was part of a model's training data
- the model's learning rate
- the GPU it was trained on
> Models tend to be more confident on training examples; such attacks quantify privacy leakage and memorization.

Q: Evaluation awareness is a concern for safety testing because [H]
- models cannot be evaluated at all
* a model that can tell it is being tested may behave differently than it would in deployment, making eval results unrepresentative
- it increases inference cost
- it reduces the context window
> Realistic test environments and interpretability checks are used to detect and reduce this gap.

Q: Constitutional or classifier-based safeguards around an LLM work by [M]
- retraining the base model for every request
* screening inputs and outputs with separate classifiers trained to detect disallowed content
- lowering the sampling temperature
- removing the system prompt
> Independent classifiers add a layer of defence against jailbreaks that does not rely on the main model's own refusals.

Q: Mechanistic interpretability "circuits" research attempts to [M]
- design new GPU hardware
* identify the specific components (heads, neurons, features) and their interactions that implement a behaviour
- increase the number of layers
- train models without data
> Techniques such as activation patching and attribution graphs localize computation by intervening on internal activations and observing the effect.
