# topic: Evaluation Metrics & Experimentation
# id: eval
# subtopics: Precision, recall, F1 and the confusion matrix; ROC AUC and PR AUC; Calibration and log loss; Regression metrics; Ranking metrics (NDCG, MAP, MRR); Class imbalance; A/B testing, power and significance; Offline versus online evaluation

Q: Precision is defined as [E]
- TP / (TP + FN)
* TP / (TP + FP)
- TN / (TN + FP)
- (TP + TN) / total
> Precision is the fraction of predicted positives that are correct. TP / (TP + FN) is recall.

Q: A classifier predicts "negative" for every example on a dataset with 1% positives. Its accuracy and recall are [E]
- 1% and 100%
* 99% and 0%
- 50% and 50%
- 99% and 100%
> Accuracy is misleading under class imbalance: the model is right 99% of the time yet finds none of the positives.

Q: The F1 score is [E]
- the arithmetic mean of precision and recall
* the harmonic mean of precision and recall
- the product of precision and recall
- the maximum of precision and recall
> F1 = 2PR / (P + R). The harmonic mean is dominated by the smaller value, so both must be high.

Q: ROC AUC can be interpreted as [M]
- the accuracy at the best threshold
* the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored higher than a randomly chosen negative
- the fraction of positives in the top decile
- the average precision
> AUC equals the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney statistic and depends only on the ranking of scores, not on their calibration.

Q: For a heavily imbalanced problem where positives are rare and of primary interest, which summary is usually more informative than ROC AUC? [M]
- Accuracy
* Precision-recall AUC (average precision)
- Specificity
- R-squared
> The false-positive rate in ROC curves is diluted by the huge number of negatives; PR curves focus on the positive class and expose poor precision.

Q: Lowering the decision threshold of a binary classifier generally [E]
- increases precision and decreases recall
* increases recall and decreases precision
- increases both
- changes neither
> More examples are predicted positive, so more true positives are found (recall up) at the cost of more false positives (precision typically down).

Q: Which metric depends on the predicted probabilities being well calibrated, not just correctly ranked? [M]
- ROC AUC
* Log loss
- Recall at k
- Spearman correlation
> Log loss and Brier score are proper scoring rules sensitive to the actual probability values; AUC is invariant to any monotonic transformation of scores.

Q: A reliability diagram plots [M]
- loss against training steps
* observed frequency of positives against predicted probability, per bin
- true-positive rate against false-positive rate
- precision against recall
> A calibrated model lies on the diagonal. Expected calibration error summarizes the gap between confidence and accuracy across bins.

Q: Which regression metric is least sensitive to outliers? [E]
- Mean squared error
- Root mean squared error
* Mean absolute error
- Maximum error
> Squared errors grow quadratically with the residual, so a few large errors dominate MSE and RMSE; MAE grows linearly.

Q: NDCG for a ranked list [M]
- ignores the position of relevant items
* discounts the gain of each item logarithmically by its rank and normalizes by the ideal ordering
- requires binary relevance labels
- is the same as precision at k
> DCG sums gain / log2(rank + 1); dividing by the DCG of the ideal ranking gives a value in [0, 1] and supports graded relevance.

Q: Mean reciprocal rank (MRR) is based on [E]
- the number of relevant items in the top k
* the rank of the first relevant item for each query
- the average relevance of all items
- the last relevant item
> MRR averages 1 / rank of the first relevant result, so it suits tasks with one correct answer such as question answering.

Q: Macro-averaged F1 differs from micro-averaged F1 in that macro averaging [M]
- pools all predictions before computing the metric
* computes F1 per class and averages with equal weight per class
- weights classes by their frequency
- can only be used for binary problems
> Macro F1 treats rare and frequent classes equally; micro F1 aggregates counts and is dominated by frequent classes.

Q: In an A/B test, statistical power is the probability of [M]
- a false positive
* detecting an effect when a true effect of the assumed size exists
- the null hypothesis being true
- the metric being normally distributed
> Power = 1 - beta. It increases with sample size, effect size and significance level, and decreases with metric variance.

Q: To detect an effect half as large with the same power and significance, the required sample size is roughly [M]
- the same
- doubled
* quadrupled
- halved
> Required n scales with 1 / (effect size)^2, so halving the minimum detectable effect needs about four times as many samples.

Q: "Peeking" at an A/B test repeatedly and stopping as soon as p < 0.05 [M]
- is good practice that saves time
* inflates the false-positive rate well above 5%
- reduces statistical power to zero
- only matters for Bayesian tests
> Each look is another chance to cross the threshold by chance. Use a fixed horizon, sequential testing or alpha-spending corrections.

Q: Randomizing by user rather than by page view in an online experiment is important because [M]
- it increases the sample size
* observations from the same user are correlated and a user should have a consistent experience
- page views cannot be logged
- it removes novelty effects
> The randomization unit should match the unit of analysis and independence; otherwise variance is underestimated and experiences are inconsistent.

Q: A model improves offline AUC but hurts the online business metric. Which is the LEAST plausible explanation? [M]
- The offline data were logged under a different policy, causing selection bias
- The offline metric is not aligned with the business objective
- Training-serving skew in feature computation
* AUC is mathematically incapable of increasing when a model improves
> Offline/online gaps commonly come from biased logged data, proxy metrics, skew, latency or feedback loops, not from any property of AUC.

Q: Cohen's kappa is used to [H]
- measure regression error
* measure agreement between annotators (or model and labels) corrected for chance agreement
- compute the area under the ROC curve
- test normality of residuals
> kappa = (p_observed - p_expected) / (1 - p_expected). It is common for inter-annotator agreement and imbalanced classification.

Q: Which is the appropriate baseline to compare against when reporting a classifier's accuracy on an imbalanced dataset? [E]
- 0%
- 50%
* The majority-class rate
- 100%
> A trivial classifier that always predicts the most frequent class achieves the majority rate, so useful models must beat it.

Q: The Matthews correlation coefficient is valued for binary classification because [H]
- it ignores true negatives
* it uses all four confusion-matrix cells and stays informative under class imbalance
- it is always between 0 and 1
- it equals accuracy for balanced data in all cases
> MCC is the correlation between predictions and labels, ranges from -1 to 1 and is high only when the classifier does well on both classes.

Q: A variance-reduction technique such as CUPED in A/B testing works by [H]
- increasing the traffic allocation
* adjusting the metric with a pre-experiment covariate correlated with it
- removing outlier users
- running the test longer
> Subtracting theta times a pre-period covariate removes variance explained before treatment, tightening confidence intervals without bias.

Q: BLEU evaluates machine translation by [M]
- human ratings
* modified n-gram precision against reference translations, with a brevity penalty
- recall of reference unigrams only
- embedding similarity
> BLEU combines clipped n-gram precisions geometrically and penalizes outputs shorter than the reference. ROUGE is recall-oriented.

Q: Perplexity of a language model on a test set is [M]
- the number of parameters divided by the number of tokens
* the exponential of the average negative log-likelihood per token
- the fraction of tokens predicted correctly
- the entropy of the vocabulary
> PPL = exp(cross-entropy). A perplexity of k corresponds to being as uncertain as a uniform choice among k tokens.

Q: When evaluating several model variants on the same test set, a paired test (for example a paired bootstrap or McNemar's test) is preferred because [H]
- it needs no data
* it accounts for the fact that both models are evaluated on the same examples, reducing variance
- it assumes the models are independent
- it requires larger test sets
> Per-example errors of two models are strongly correlated; analysing paired differences gives much tighter comparisons than treating results as independent samples.

Q: Simpson's paradox in experiment analysis refers to [H]
- p-values falling as sample size grows
* a trend appearing in every subgroup that reverses when the subgroups are combined
- the winner's curse in metric selection
- regression to the mean
> Unequal group sizes across segments can reverse aggregated comparisons; check sample-ratio mismatch and segment mixes.
