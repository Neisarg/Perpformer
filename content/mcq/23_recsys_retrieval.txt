# topic: Recommender Systems & Retrieval
# id: recsys
# subtopics: Collaborative filtering and matrix factorization; Two-tower retrieval and approximate nearest neighbours; Ranking models and learning to rank; Negative sampling and in-batch softmax; Cold start and exploration; Position and selection bias; Multi-stage pipelines; Embedding tables and feature crosses

Q: Collaborative filtering makes recommendations based on [E]
- item content features only
* patterns in user-item interactions across many users
- the user's demographic profile only
- random sampling
> Users who behaved similarly in the past are assumed to share preferences; no item content is required.

Q: Matrix factorization for recommendations approximates the user-item matrix as [E]
- a sum of two matrices
* the product of low-dimensional user and item embedding matrices
- a diagonal matrix
- a decision tree per user
> The predicted score is the dot product of a user vector and an item vector learned from observed interactions.

Q: The cold-start problem refers to [E]
- slow model inference
* difficulty recommending for new users or new items that have little or no interaction history
- low GPU temperature
- training divergence at the first step
> Content features, popularity priors, onboarding questions and exploration help until enough interactions accumulate.

Q: Large-scale recommender systems use a multi-stage pipeline (retrieval, ranking, re-ranking) because [M]
- a single model cannot be trained
* scoring every item with a heavy model is too expensive, so cheap models narrow millions of items to hundreds before precise ranking
- regulations require three models
- it removes the need for features
> Candidate generation optimizes recall at low cost; the ranker uses rich features for precision; re-ranking applies diversity and business rules.

Q: In a two-tower retrieval model, the user and item towers interact [M]
- at every layer through cross-attention
* only at the end, through a dot product or cosine similarity of their embeddings
- through a shared decision tree
- not at all
> Late interaction lets item embeddings be precomputed and indexed for approximate nearest-neighbour search.

Q: In-batch negative sampling for two-tower training introduces a bias because [H]
- negatives are too easy to compute
* popular items appear as negatives more often, so they are over-penalized; a logQ correction compensates
- positives are missing
- the batch is too small to compute gradients
> Items are sampled in proportion to their frequency; subtracting the log of the sampling probability from the logits corrects the sampled softmax.

Q: Implicit feedback (clicks, watches) differs from explicit feedback (ratings) in that [M]
- it is scarcer
* it is abundant but noisy and has no true negatives, since not interacting may mean not seeing
- it cannot be used for training
- it is always unbiased
> Unobserved items are a mix of dislikes and non-exposure, so training uses sampled negatives and confidence weighting.

Q: Position bias in click logs means [M]
- items are stored in the wrong position
* users click higher-ranked results more often regardless of relevance
- embeddings depend on position encodings
- ranking models ignore order
> Training on raw clicks would reinforce the existing ranking; inverse propensity weighting or a position feature used only in training mitigates this.

Q: A pairwise learning-to-rank loss such as RankNet or BPR optimizes [M]
- the absolute score of each item
* the relative order of pairs of items for the same query or user
- the full permutation in one step
- the number of items retrieved
> Pointwise losses predict a label per item, pairwise losses order pairs, and listwise losses (such as ListNet or LambdaRank-style objectives) consider the whole list.

Q: Hashing categorical ids into a fixed number of embedding rows (the "hashing trick") trades [M]
- accuracy for interpretability
* memory for collisions, where unrelated ids share an embedding
- training speed for inference speed
- recall for precision exactly
> It bounds table size for very high-cardinality features; multiple hashes or frequency-based allocation reduce the harm of collisions.

Q: A feedback loop in a recommender system arises because [M]
- gradients flow backwards
* the model's own recommendations determine what users see and click, which becomes the next training data
- users give explicit ratings
- the model is retrained too rarely
> This can amplify popularity and narrow exposure; exploration and counterfactual corrections keep the data informative.

Q: Wide & Deep models combine [M]
- two identical deep networks
* a linear model over cross-product features (memorization) with a deep network over embeddings (generalization)
- a CNN and an RNN
- matrix factorization with k-means
> The wide part memorizes specific feature co-occurrences; the deep part generalizes to unseen combinations.

Q: For a retrieval stage, the most appropriate offline metric is [M]
- NDCG@1 of the final ranking
* recall@k: the fraction of relevant items that appear in the candidate set
- training loss of the ranker
- AUC of click prediction
> Retrieval must not miss relevant items; ordering within the candidate set is the ranker's job.

Q: Product quantization in approximate nearest-neighbour search [H]
- increases embedding dimension
* splits each vector into sub-vectors and replaces each by the id of its nearest codebook centroid, compressing memory dramatically
- sorts vectors by norm
- removes the need for an index
> Distances are approximated from small lookup tables; combined with an inverted file (IVF-PQ) it scales to billions of vectors.

Q: Maximum inner product search (MIPS) differs from cosine similarity search in that [H]
- it ignores vector direction
* vector norms affect the score, so items with large norms can dominate unless embeddings are normalized
- it cannot use approximate indexes
- it requires binary vectors
> Inner product equals cosine times the norms; many ANN libraries reduce MIPS to nearest-neighbour search through normalization or augmentation tricks.

Q: Calibration matters for an ads click-through-rate model because [M]
- only the ranking of ads matters
* predicted probabilities feed into auctions and bids (expected value = bid x pCTR), so systematic over- or under-prediction costs money
- it reduces training time
- it removes position bias
> Unlike pure ranking, pricing and budget pacing depend on the absolute probability values.

Q: Multi-task ranking models (for example predicting click, like and watch time together) often use architectures such as MMoE to [H]
- reduce the number of tasks
* share representation through several experts with task-specific gates, limiting negative transfer between conflicting tasks
- avoid using embeddings
- train each task on a separate dataset with no shared parameters
> Gating lets each task weight the shared experts differently instead of forcing one shared bottom.

Q: Sequential recommenders such as SASRec model a user by [M]
- a single static embedding from matrix factorization
* applying self-attention over the user's ordered interaction history to predict the next item
- averaging item popularity
- clustering users with k-means
> Treating item ids like tokens captures short-term intent and order effects that static factorization misses.

Q: Exploration in recommender systems is necessary because [M]
- users dislike relevant items
* without showing uncertain items the system never collects the data needed to learn their true value
- it lowers serving cost
- it makes the model deterministic
> Bandit-style strategies (epsilon-greedy, Thompson sampling) trade short-term engagement for information that improves future recommendations.

Q: Which is a common cause of training-serving skew in recommender systems? [M]
- Using GPUs for training
* Features computed differently (or at a different time) offline and online, such as using future information in training logs
- Too many negative samples
- Using embeddings
> Logging the features at serving time and reusing them for training, or a shared feature store, keeps the two paths consistent.

Q: Diversity-aware re-ranking methods such as maximal marginal relevance (MMR) select items by [M]
- relevance only
* balancing relevance with dissimilarity to items already selected
- random shuffling
- item price
> MMR greedily picks the item maximizing lambda * relevance - (1 - lambda) * max similarity to the chosen set.

Q: The alternating least squares (ALS) algorithm for matrix factorization works by [H]
- stochastic gradient descent on both factor matrices at once
* fixing the item factors and solving a least-squares problem for the user factors, then alternating
- eigendecomposition of the rating matrix
- sampling from a posterior
> With one side fixed the objective is quadratic and has a closed-form ridge solution per user (or item), which parallelizes well.

Q: Semantic IDs for generative retrieval represent each item as [H]
- a random integer
* a short sequence of discrete codes derived from its content embedding (for example by residual quantization), which a sequence model generates
- a one-hot vector over all items
- its title text only
> Similar items share code prefixes, so an autoregressive model can generalize to new items and retrieve by decoding codes.

Q: When evaluating a new ranker offline on logs collected by the old ranker, inverse propensity scoring is used to [H]
- speed up evaluation
* correct for the fact that the old policy chose which items were shown
- remove duplicates
- increase the number of positives
> Counterfactual estimators reweight logged outcomes by the inverse probability that the logging policy showed the item.

Q: Dwell time or watch time is often preferred to clicks as a training signal because [M]
- it is easier to log
* clicks alone reward clickbait, while time spent better reflects satisfaction
- it is binary
- it has no noise
> Optimizing a proxy shapes the product; engagement-quality signals and explicit feedback reduce clickbait incentives.
