# topic: LLM Concepts
# id: llm
# subtopics: Pretraining objectives and scaling; Sampling and decoding (temperature, top-k, top-p); Prompting, in-context learning and chain of thought; Retrieval-augmented generation; Post-training (SFT, RLHF, DPO, GRPO, RLVR); Hallucination, calibration and evaluation; Context windows, structured output and prompt caching; Multimodal LLMs; Safety and prompt injection

Q: Decoder-only LLMs such as GPT are pretrained with which objective? [E]
- Masked language modeling
* Next-token prediction (causal language modeling)
- Contrastive image-text matching
- Denoising of shuffled sentences
> The model maximizes the likelihood of each token given all preceding tokens.

Q: Raising the sampling temperature above 1 [E]
- makes the output deterministic
* flattens the next-token distribution, increasing diversity and randomness
- sharpens the distribution toward the most likely token
- changes the model's weights
> Logits are divided by T before the softmax. T < 1 sharpens the distribution; T approaching 0 gives greedy decoding.

Q: Nucleus (top-p) sampling with p = 0.9 samples from [M]
- the 90 most likely tokens
* the smallest set of most likely tokens whose cumulative probability reaches 0.9, renormalized
- tokens whose individual probability exceeds 0.9
- the least likely 10% of tokens
> The candidate set adapts to the shape of the distribution: small when the model is confident, large when it is uncertain. Top-k uses a fixed-size set.

Q: In-context learning refers to [E]
- fine-tuning on a small dataset
* the model performing a new task from instructions or examples in the prompt, with no weight updates
- learning during the pretraining phase only
- retrieval from a vector database
> Few-shot examples condition the forward pass; the parameters are unchanged.

Q: Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) primarily helps with [E]
- reducing the model's parameter count
* grounding answers in external, up-to-date or private documents supplied in the context
- making the tokenizer faster
- eliminating the need for a prompt
> Relevant passages are retrieved (often by embedding similarity) and inserted into the prompt, reducing hallucination and adding knowledge without retraining.

Q: In a RAG pipeline, a cross-encoder reranker is used after first-stage retrieval because [M]
- it is faster than a bi-encoder
* it scores each query-document pair jointly and more accurately, but is too slow to run over the whole corpus
- it removes the need for embeddings
- it compresses documents
> Bi-encoders embed queries and documents separately for fast approximate search; a cross-encoder then re-scores the top candidates with full attention between query and passage.

Q: Hybrid search in RAG combines [M]
- two different LLMs
* sparse lexical retrieval (such as BM25) with dense embedding retrieval
- fine-tuning with prompting
- images with text
> Lexical search handles exact terms, identifiers and rare words; dense search captures paraphrase. Their rankings are fused, for example with reciprocal rank fusion.

Q: RLHF with PPO, as used for early chat models, involves [M]
- supervised training on human-written answers only
* training a reward model from human preference comparisons, then optimizing the policy against it with a KL penalty to stay near the original model
- pruning the model using human feedback
- asking humans to label each token
> Pairwise preferences train the reward model; PPO then maximizes predicted reward, with a KL term preventing drift from the supervised policy.

Q: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) differs from PPO-based RLHF in that it [H]
- needs no preference data
* optimizes the policy directly on preference pairs with a classification-style loss, without a separate reward model or RL rollouts
- requires an online environment
- trains the reward model twice
> DPO derives a closed-form loss from the KL-constrained reward maximization objective: it raises the log-probability ratio of preferred over rejected responses relative to a frozen reference model.

Q: GRPO, used for training reasoning models with verifiable rewards, estimates the advantage of a sampled response by [H]
- a learned value network
* comparing its reward with the mean (and standard deviation) of rewards of a group of responses sampled for the same prompt
- human ratings of each token
- the length of the response
> Group Relative Policy Optimization drops the critic: several completions per prompt are scored, and rewards normalized within the group serve as advantages in a PPO-style clipped objective.

Q: "Hallucination" in LLMs refers to [E]
- slow generation
* fluent output that is not supported by the provided context or by facts
- refusing to answer
- repeating the prompt
> The model generates plausible text because it is trained to predict likely continuations, not to verify truth. Grounding, citations and abstention training mitigate it.

Q: Chain-of-thought prompting improves accuracy on multi-step problems mainly because [M]
- it increases the temperature
* intermediate reasoning tokens give the model serial computation and a scratchpad to condition on
- it shortens the prompt
- it changes the tokenizer
> A Transformer does a bounded amount of computation per token; writing out steps lets later tokens attend to earlier intermediate results.

Q: Self-consistency decoding [M]
- runs greedy decoding once
* samples several reasoning paths and returns the most common final answer
- averages the logits of two models
- checks grammar
> Majority voting over diverse sampled chains marginalizes out individual reasoning errors, at the cost of more inference.

Q: Constrained (structured) decoding guarantees valid JSON by [M]
- asking politely in the prompt
* masking, at each step, tokens that would violate a grammar or schema before sampling
- fine-tuning on JSON only
- post-processing with regular expressions after generation
> A grammar or finite-state machine tracks the valid next tokens; invalid logits are set to -inf so the output always parses.

Q: Prompt caching reduces cost and latency by [M]
- storing the final answers to frequent questions
* reusing the computed attention states (KV cache) of a shared prompt prefix across requests
- compressing the prompt with gzip
- lowering the temperature
> If many requests share a long prefix (system prompt, documents, tool definitions), its internal states are computed once; only the new suffix is processed. Any change early in the prefix invalidates the cache.

Q: The "lost in the middle" effect describes [M]
- tokens dropped by the tokenizer
* models using information at the beginning and end of a long context more reliably than information in the middle
- gradients vanishing in middle layers
- middle layers being pruned
> Retrieval accuracy within the context is often U-shaped in position, which motivates ordering the most relevant passages first or last and keeping context focused.

Q: Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) of a chat model on instruction-response pairs typically computes the loss on [M]
- all tokens including the user prompt
* the assistant's response tokens only, masking the prompt tokens
- the first token of each turn
- the special tokens only
> Loss masking focuses learning on generating responses given prompts; training on the prompt would teach the model to imitate users.

Q: Why do LLMs struggle with exact arithmetic on long numbers or counting letters in words? [M]
- They have too few parameters
* Tokenization splits numbers and words into irregular subword pieces and the model has no built-in algorithmic working memory
- The softmax cannot output digits
- They are trained without numbers
> Characters are hidden inside multi-character tokens and carries require long exact serial computation; tools such as a code interpreter solve this reliably.

Q: A vision-language model like LLaVA feeds images to the LLM by [M]
- converting the image to a text caption with OCR only
* encoding the image with a vision encoder and projecting patch features into the LLM's token embedding space
- training a separate LLM per image
- concatenating raw pixel values to the logits
> A pretrained vision encoder (often CLIP or SigLIP) produces patch embeddings; a projector maps them to "visual tokens" that the LLM attends to like text.

Q: LLM-as-a-judge evaluation should be validated by [M]
- trusting the judge with no checks
* measuring agreement with human ratings and checking for biases such as position and verbosity preference
- using the same model output as the reference
- increasing the judge's temperature
> Judges show systematic biases (order, length, self-preference). Calibrate against human labels, randomize order and use clear rubrics.

Q: Indirect prompt injection is [M]
- a user typing a jailbreak directly
* malicious instructions hidden in content the model reads, such as a web page, email or tool result
- an attack on the tokenizer
- a way to reduce latency
> The model cannot reliably separate data from instructions, so untrusted content can hijack an application; defences include privilege separation, filtering and requiring confirmation for sensitive actions.

Q: Scaling laws for neural language models (Kaplan et al., Chinchilla) indicate that, for a fixed compute budget, [H]
- only model size matters
* model size and training tokens should be scaled together; many earlier models were under-trained for their size
- data should be held fixed while parameters grow
- the loss does not depend on compute
> Chinchilla found roughly equal scaling of parameters and tokens (about 20 tokens per parameter) to be compute-optimal.

Q: "Reasoning" or extended-thinking models differ from standard chat models at inference in that they [M]
- use a different tokenizer
* generate a (possibly hidden) chain of intermediate reasoning tokens before the final answer, trading latency and cost for accuracy
- do not use attention
- cannot call tools
> Test-time compute is spent on deliberate reasoning, often trained with reinforcement learning on verifiable tasks; the thinking budget is an explicit knob.

Q: When chunking documents for RAG, chunks that are too large tend to [M]
- increase retrieval precision
* dilute the embedding with multiple topics and waste context window, while chunks that are too small lose necessary context
- break the tokenizer
- make embeddings longer
> Chunk size and overlap balance retrievability against completeness; structure-aware splitting and adding document-level context to each chunk help.

Q: The context window of an LLM is [E]
- the number of layers
* the maximum number of tokens (prompt plus generated output) the model can attend to at once
- the size of its vocabulary
- the batch size used during training
> Everything the model conditions on must fit in this window; longer contexts cost more compute and can degrade attention to relevant details.
