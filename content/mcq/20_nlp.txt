# topic: NLP Fundamentals
# id: nlp
# subtopics: Tokenization (BPE, WordPiece, SentencePiece); Word embeddings (word2vec, GloVe); TF-IDF and BM25; Language modeling and smoothing; Decoding strategies; Sentence embeddings and semantic search; Sequence labeling and classification

Q: Byte-pair encoding (BPE) builds its vocabulary by [E]
- splitting on whitespace only
* repeatedly merging the most frequent adjacent pair of symbols
- hashing words into buckets
- keeping the most frequent whole words
> Starting from characters (or bytes), each merge adds one new token, so frequent words become single tokens and rare words split into pieces.

Q: The main advantage of subword tokenization over word-level tokenization is [E]
- shorter sequences in all cases
* an open vocabulary: any word can be represented as known pieces, avoiding out-of-vocabulary tokens
- no need for an embedding table
- exact preservation of grammar
> Subwords balance vocabulary size against sequence length and handle rare words, typos and morphology.

Q: word2vec's skip-gram model is trained to [E]
- predict the next sentence
* predict surrounding context words from a centre word
- reconstruct a document's topic
- classify sentiment
> CBOW does the reverse, predicting the centre word from its context. Both learn embeddings in which words in similar contexts are close.

Q: Negative sampling in word2vec replaces the full softmax with [M]
- a hierarchical tree
* a binary classification between the true context word and a few randomly sampled "noise" words
- beam search
- an exact partition function
> It avoids normalizing over the whole vocabulary: each step updates the positive pair and k negatives only.

Q: A key limitation of static embeddings such as word2vec and GloVe is that [E]
- they cannot be trained on large corpora
* each word has a single vector regardless of context, so different senses of a word are conflated
- they have too many dimensions
- they require labeled data
> "bank" gets one vector for both the river and the financial sense; contextual models (ELMo, BERT) produce context-dependent representations.

Q: In TF-IDF, the inverse document frequency term [E]
- increases the weight of words that appear in many documents
* decreases the weight of words that appear in many documents
- counts the words in a document
- normalizes for document length
> IDF = log(N / df) down-weights common terms such as "the" and emphasizes terms that discriminate between documents.

Q: BM25 improves on plain TF-IDF by [M]
- using neural embeddings
* saturating term frequency and normalizing for document length
- ignoring rare terms
- requiring labeled relevance data
> The k1 parameter limits the benefit of repeated terms and b controls length normalization, making BM25 a strong lexical baseline.

Q: An n-gram language model needs smoothing because [M]
- it has too few parameters
* many valid n-grams never appear in the training data and would otherwise get zero probability
- it cannot count words
- it uses subword tokens
> Techniques such as Kneser-Ney redistribute probability mass to unseen events and back off to lower-order statistics.

Q: Greedy decoding and beam search with large beams tend to produce text that is [M]
- highly random and incoherent
* generic and repetitive for open-ended generation, despite high likelihood
- always factually correct
- longer than sampled text
> Maximum-likelihood decoding suits tasks with a narrow set of correct outputs (translation). For open-ended text, sampling methods give more human-like diversity.

Q: A length penalty is applied in beam search because [M]
- long sequences are always better
* summing log-probabilities favours shorter hypotheses, since each added token lowers the total score
- it prevents repetition
- it speeds up decoding
> Normalizing the score by a function of length counteracts the bias toward short outputs.

Q: To obtain a sentence embedding for semantic search from a BERT-like encoder, a strong approach is [M]
- using the raw [CLS] vector of an off-the-shelf masked language model
* training with a contrastive objective on sentence pairs and mean-pooling the token representations
- summing one-hot vectors
- using the last token's embedding from the tokenizer
> Without contrastive fine-tuning (as in Sentence-BERT or SimCSE), BERT's sentence vectors are poorly suited to cosine similarity.

Q: Approximate nearest-neighbour indexes such as HNSW are used in semantic search to [M]
- improve embedding quality
* find close vectors in sub-linear time, trading a small amount of recall for speed
- tokenize queries
- compress text
> HNSW navigates a multi-layer proximity graph; IVF and product quantization are other common choices for large corpora.

Q: WordPiece (used in BERT) marks subword continuation with [M]
- a leading underscore
* a "##" prefix on pieces that continue a word
- a trailing space
- capital letters
> SentencePiece instead marks word starts with a special underscore-like symbol and works directly on raw text, including spaces.

Q: Named-entity recognition is usually formulated as [E]
- sentence classification
* token-level sequence labeling, for example with BIO tags
- language modeling
- clustering
> Each token gets a label marking the Beginning, Inside or Outside of an entity span; a CRF layer can enforce valid tag transitions.

Q: A large vocabulary increases the cost of language model training mainly because of [M]
- the attention layers
* the output softmax over all vocabulary items and the size of the embedding matrices
- the positional encodings
- the normalization layers
> The final projection and normalization scale with vocabulary size; sampled softmax, adaptive softmax and subword vocabularies reduce the cost.

Q: Repetition penalties and no-repeat n-gram blocking are decoding heuristics that address [M]
- slow tokenization
* degenerate loops in which the model repeats phrases
- vanishing gradients
- out-of-vocabulary words
> Likelihood-maximizing decoders can get stuck in repetition; these heuristics down-weight or forbid recently generated tokens.

Q: The distributional hypothesis underlying word embeddings states that [E]
- words are distributed uniformly
* words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings
- grammar determines meaning
- every word has exactly one meaning
> "You shall know a word by the company it keeps": co-occurrence statistics carry semantic information.

Q: Cosine similarity is preferred to Euclidean distance for comparing text embeddings mainly because [M]
- it is faster to compute
* it ignores vector magnitude, which often reflects frequency or length rather than meaning
- it works only for sparse vectors
- it satisfies the triangle inequality
> Direction carries the semantics; for unit-normalized vectors cosine and Euclidean rankings coincide.

Q: The analogy "king - man + woman is close to queen" illustrates that word embeddings [M]
- memorize dictionary definitions
* encode some semantic relations as approximately linear offsets
- are binary vectors
- cannot represent gender
> Vector arithmetic recovers many relational patterns, though the effect is weaker than early demonstrations suggested.

Q: Byte-level BPE, as used by GPT-2 and later models, guarantees that [M]
- every word is one token
* any Unicode string can be tokenized without unknown tokens, since the base vocabulary is the 256 byte values
- tokens never cross word boundaries
- the vocabulary has exactly 256 entries
> Falling back to bytes covers every input; merges then build larger units on top.

Q: Typical English text averages roughly how many tokens per word with common subword tokenizers? [M]
- 0.25
- 0.75
* 1.3
- 4
> A common rule of thumb is about 4 characters or 0.75 words per token, that is around 1.3 tokens per word; code and other languages differ.

Q: A conditional random field (CRF) layer on top of a sequence encoder adds [H]
- attention between tokens
* learned transition scores between adjacent labels, with exact inference by the Viterbi algorithm
- subword tokenization
- a language-model loss
> The CRF scores whole label sequences, preventing invalid sequences such as an "inside" tag without a preceding "begin".

Q: Matryoshka representation learning trains embeddings so that [H]
- each dimension is binary
* prefixes of the vector (for example the first 64, 256 or 1024 dimensions) are themselves useful embeddings
- vectors are nested inside tokens
- the embedding size grows during inference
> The loss is applied at several truncation lengths, letting applications trade accuracy for storage and speed by truncating.

Q: Late-interaction retrieval models such as ColBERT score a query-document pair by [H]
- one dot product between two pooled vectors
* summing, over query tokens, the maximum similarity to any document token
- concatenating the texts and running a classifier
- counting shared words
> Keeping per-token embeddings and using MaxSim is more expressive than single-vector bi-encoders while documents can still be indexed offline.

Q: Label-free pretraining of text encoders with "replaced token detection" (ELECTRA) trains the model to [H]
- predict masked tokens from a vocabulary softmax
* decide for every token whether it is original or was replaced by a small generator
- order shuffled sentences
- translate the sentence
> Because the loss is defined on all tokens instead of the 15% that are masked, it is more sample-efficient than masked language modeling.
